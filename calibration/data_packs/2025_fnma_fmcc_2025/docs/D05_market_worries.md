Title: Political enthusiasm does not settle seniority, dilution or mortgage costs
Source: compiled from: https://www.cnn.com/2024/12/02/economy/fannie-mae-freddie-mac-mortgage-rates-housing/index.html; https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2767; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/310522/000031052225000004/fnm-20250102.htm; https://x.com/BillAckman/status/1873818034428694837
Date: 2025-01-15

CNN's December 2 report says privatization is widely expected under the incoming Trump administration, but warns that changing the government relationship could unsettle mortgage investors and increase borrowing costs. That is a policy tradeoff: maximizing the share value of the enterprises is not necessarily the government's sole objective.

No new, directly authenticated pre-January15 Trump promise specifying cancellation of senior preferred, warrant terms or a relisting date is recovered here. Expectations based on the first administration's unfinished reform effort must not be represented as an enacted second-term plan.

The January 2 Treasury/FHFA amendments restore Treasury's consent right over release from conservatorship. The official release states that FHFA will seek public input about the potential effects on housing and financial markets. These requirements can make a release more orderly, but also add process, negotiation and timing uncertainty.

Common holders face several adverse scenarios. Treasury might insist on preserving or converting its senior preferred claim, causing large dilution. A high regulatory capital requirement might require substantial outside equity. A continuing guarantee could require fees that reduce earnings. Political disagreement over housing affordability could delay reform even while the companies remain profitable.

The retained-earnings story also needs care. Accounting net worth is increasing, but current contracts can increase Treasury's liquidation preference alongside retained earnings. A growing balance sheet is therefore not by itself proof of increasing distributable common value.

Ackman himself acknowledged uncertainty in his December 30 post: “There remains a high degree of uncertainty about the ultimate outcome so you should limit your exposure to what you can afford to lose if you choose to invest.” That warning is part of the public thesis, not a disclaimer to be discarded because his upside estimate is attractive.

The constructive case is that profitable franchises and Treasury's potential warrant proceeds align shareholder and taxpayer incentives for a negotiated solution. The bear case is that the sovereign controls the terms and can choose a path with limited value for existing common. Both remain live at the cutoff; the rally does not resolve them.
