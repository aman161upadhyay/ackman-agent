Title: An aerospace franchise facing a product-safety interruption
Source: compiled from: https://investors.boeing.com/investors/news/press-release-details/2019/Boeing-Reports-Record-2018-Results-and-Provides-2019-Guidance/default.aspx; https://www.zeebiz.com/agencies/boeing-prepares-for-max-737-jets-sitting-outside-factory-as-deliveries-halted-90208
Date: 2019-03-15

Boeing designs and manufactures commercial aircraft, defense and space systems, and provides related services. Its commercial franchise benefits from engineering scale, certification requirements, a global supplier network and airlines' installed fleets. Airbus is the principal competitor in large commercial aircraft.

The company's total backlog at December2018 is approximatelyUS$490 billion, including nearly5,900 commercial aircraft. Backlog provides visibility into potential deliveries but is not immediately available cash or an unconditional claim on future profit. Customer financing, cancellations, production schedules and certification affect conversion.

The737 program serves the important single-aisle market. The MAX is its newest major version. A common fleet can help airlines with operating familiarity, training and maintenance, but safety confidence and regulatory permission are prerequisites for revenue service.

Following the March10 Ethiopian Airlines crash and the earlier Lion Air crash, authorities worldwide have grounded the MAX. Reuters's March15 account reports Boeing has suspended MAX deliveries while continuing production. The accident causes remain under investigation at this date.

The Reuters report says the broader737 line is producing52 aircraft per month and that MAX constitutes most output. This should not be misrepresented as52 delivered MAX aircraft per month after grounding. Physical production and cash delivery are now different.

Aircraft manufacturing has substantial working-capital commitments. Suppliers, labor and assembly costs may continue while customer delivery payments stop. Inventory accumulation can therefore impair cash conversion even before any final legal or engineering cost is known.

Defense and services provide other earnings streams, but they do not make the commercial interruption immaterial. Consolidated valuation must separate those businesses and their obligations from the temporarily unmarketable product.

The durable-franchise argument is strong: few companies can produce commercial aircraft at this scale, and demand for replacement/capacity aircraft has supported a large order book. The competing concern is whether the duration, technical remedy, certification process and customer response can be forecast with adequate confidence.

At the cutoff, no definitive common global return-to-service schedule or comprehensive cost estimate is available. The investment is not simply a familiar industrial cycle; it also requires an assessment of unresolved safety, regulatory and legal outcomes.
