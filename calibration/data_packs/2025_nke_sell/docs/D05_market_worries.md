Title: Repair costs, tariffs and whether former margins remain attainable

Date: 2025-10-01

Reuters' October 1 report, syndicated by CNN, frames the quarter positively: turnaround efforts gained traction, revenue unexpectedly rose and shares gained more than 3%. It also identifies China weakness and a $1.5 billion annualized tariff bill. An honest market account must preserve the encouraging operating signs rather than write only a bearish narrative.

The central risk is that repairing prior strategic choices requires more time and margin sacrifice than originally expected. Inventory cleanup, wholesale incentives, marketing and sport-focused innovation all consume resources. Wholesale shipment growth is constructive, but it is not a full measure of end-consumer demand or profitability.

Direct revenue and digital remain weak. Management does not expect Nike Direct to grow during fiscal 2026 while restoring full-price economics. That means a large channel is still undergoing a deliberate reset rather than returning immediately to the old trajectory.

China is another independent constraint. Q1 Greater China revenue declined 9%, and management expects regional revenue/margin pressure through fiscal 2026. Competitive alternatives and local consumer preferences could make recovery harder even if global brand equity remains substantial.

Supply Chain Dive's July 2 account describes shifting sourcing away from China and selective US price increases. It reports the June plan to reduce Chinese-origin US footwear imports from roughly 16% to a high-single-digit share by fiscal year-end. This is a planned mitigation, not proof it has already eliminated tariff exposure.

Tariffs may be more structural than temporary inventory discounts. Supplier negotiations, sourcing changes and pricing can soften the impact, but could alter product economics or demand. Old gross-margin peaks should therefore be treated as a scenario to test, not an entitlement.

Competition is visible in peers' growth and margins, detailed in D06. A globally dominant brand can lose share in high-growth niches if innovation and distribution lag. The bear case is slower or lower earnings recovery, not necessarily disappearance of the brand.

Counterevidence includes positive North American growth, wholesale recovery, lower inventory, substantial liquidity and Hill's relevant operating experience. The decision turns on whether those observations sufficiently narrow the earnings-outcome range. Neither a recognized trademark nor a large stock decline resolves the forecastability question.
