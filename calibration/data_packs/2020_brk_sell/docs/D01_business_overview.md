Title: Insurance-funded conglomerate and capital allocation
Source: compiled from: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2019ar/2019ar.pdf; https://berkshirehathaway.com/qtrly/1stqtr20.pdf
Date: 2020-05-02

Berkshire combines insurance underwriting and investment operations with controlled railroad, utility, manufacturing, distribution, service and retail companies. It is not economically equivalent to a closed-end stock fund. Shareholders own operating cash flows and an insurance balance sheet alongside public securities.

Insurance includes GEICO, reinsurance and primary insurers. Premiums collected before claims are paid create float. If underwriting is profitable, that funding has a negative explicit underwriting cost; if catastrophe or reserve losses occur, the economics can reverse. Float is a liability funding investments, not unencumbered shareholder cash.

BNSF is a large freight railroad with physical network barriers and capital needs. Berkshire Hathaway Energy owns regulated utilities and other energy assets. Manufacturing/service/retail operations include industrial businesses exposed to economic activity. Subsidiary autonomy coexists with centralized allocation of significant capital.

Warren Buffett is chairman/CEO, with Charlie Munger as vice chairman. The organizational structure assigns Ajit Jain oversight of insurance and Greg Abel oversight of non-insurance operations. This helps separate operating oversight from Buffett's investment and capital-allocation role; succession remains a governance consideration.

The Q1 release reports the following after-tax operating-earnings contributions in $millions:

| Business category | Q1 2020 |
|---|---:|
| Insurance underwriting | 363 |
| Insurance investment income | 1,386 |
| Railroad/utilities/energy | 1,751 |
| Other businesses | 2,038 |
| Other | 333 |
| Total operating earnings | 5,871 |

The public-equity portfolio creates large mark-to-market changes in GAAP earnings. Those changes are separate from the above operating measure and should not be treated as a deterioration of identical size in subsidiary trading profits.

At March 31, reported shares outstanding were 696,338 Class A and 1,385,527,870 Class B. One A share has the economic interest of 1,500 B shares. The two classes have different voting power, and a Class A market quote must not be mislabeled as a Class B execution price.

Berkshire's scale is both an advantage and a constraint. It can supply unusual amounts of capital and endure stress, but moving a meaningful portion of a very large cash reserve requires very large opportunities. A shareholder must assess both the durability of existing earnings and the likely return on capital not yet deployed.
