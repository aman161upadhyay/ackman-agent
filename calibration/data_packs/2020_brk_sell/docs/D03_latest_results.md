Title: May 2 results and Buffett's pandemic capital-allocation stance
Source: compiled from: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/may0220.pdf; https://berkshirehathaway.com/qtrly/1stqtr20.pdf; https://www.cnbc.com/2020/05/02/warren-buffett-berkshire-hathaway-annual-meeting-live-updates.html; https://www.cnbc.com/2020/05/02/warren-buffett-says-berkshire-sold-its-entire-position-in-airlines-because-of-the-coronavirus.html
Date: 2020-05-04

Berkshire released Q1 results May 2 and held a virtual annual meeting the same day. Operating earnings were $5.871 billion, up from $5.555 billion. The $49.746 billion attributable GAAP loss largely reflects investment marks. The release warns investors against treating quarterly investment gains/losses as a meaningful measure of ongoing operating performance.

Insurance underwriting earned $363 million after tax; insurance investment income was $1.386 billion. Railroad/utilities/energy contributed $1.751 billion, down from $1.858 billion. Other operating businesses contributed $2.038 billion, down from $2.200 billion. The increase in the total therefore does not mean every subsidiary was growing.

The quarterly report says the pandemic began materially affecting operations in March. The quarter contains only a short period of widespread US disruption, so it is not a complete run-rate picture of shutdown conditions. Manufacturing, services, retail and transport have differing exposures; insurance claims and investment income add separate uncertainties.

The meeting disclosed a complete sale of the airline stakes in April. CNBC quotes Buffett: “The world has changed for the airlines.” The prior investments included United, American, Southwest and Delta. This is a reassessment of industry economics, not merely a decision to tolerate a temporary share-price fall.

CNBC's results coverage also reports a record roughly $137 billion cash reserve and limited stock buying during the March rout. The large cash balance provides resilience and future optionality, while raising the question of what return shareholders should expect while it remains in low-yielding instruments.

The annual meeting does not provide a precise near-term earnings forecast or an acquisition commitment. Berkshire's willingness to wait may be prudent under extreme uncertainty, but a shareholder originally valuing cash deployment must update the likely timing and scale of that deployment.

The central tension is therefore not solvency. It is the combination of a resilient conglomerate with slower immediate deployment than a smaller outside investor might choose. A concentrated shareholder can retain exposure to Berkshire's patience and selection, or own individual franchises directly; the latest results do not resolve that opportunity-cost comparison by themselves.
