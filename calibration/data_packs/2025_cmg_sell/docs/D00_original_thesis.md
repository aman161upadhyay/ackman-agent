Title: The original 2016 food-safety recovery underwriting
Source: https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2022/10/07154952/2016-Annual-Report-Letter-Only.pdf
Date: 2017-03-28

Pershing's 2016 annual letter explains a 9.9% Chipotle stake at an average $405 per share. That is before the June 2024 fifty-for-one split: the comparable split-adjusted cost is $8.10, calculated. This original purchase is distinct from the cost and size of any residual position after subsequent transactions.

The letter's central formulation is: “We have always believed that a good time to buy a great business is when it is in temporary trouble.” Food-safety incidents beginning in late 2015 had damaged sales and reputation. The underwriting was that enhanced protocols plus operational, marketing, technology and governance improvements could restore the business rather than merely engineer a short-lived stock rebound.

The letter says “all of the key drivers of Chipotle’s powerful economic moat and long-term success remain intact.” It identifies a relevant brand, differentiated food and customer value, strong restaurant economics, high returns on capital and throughput. It also cites new restaurants, mobile ordering and catering as opportunities.

A short exact extract describes “Strong unit economics and extremely high returns on capital, driven by a well-honed model that facilitates best-in-class throughput.” This is important because durable unit economics, not just comparable-sales recovery, supported the ability to reinvest at attractive returns.

The product-value argument was explicit: “The product price point offers outstanding value given the quality and quantity of food served.” Affordability and consumer perception were therefore original underwriting pillars, not a response invented for the current slowdown.

The original letter also saw room to strengthen execution and governance. The subsequent appointment of Brian Niccol was not already completed at the initial 2016 purchase; management milestones should not be moved backward in time. The initial investment required a credible improvement path, not a claim that the company already operated flawlessly.

The current review should test brand relevance, transaction frequency, throughput, restaurant returns, reinvestment runway and management execution. Strong realized historical returns would not prove the current growth path is predictable, while weak recent traffic would not by itself prove permanent brand destruction.

No precise initial PSH NAV-at-cost percentage is authenticated in this source extract. The 9.9% figure is ownership of Chipotle, not portfolio weight. Those denominators must remain separate.
