Title: The explicit zero-equity case and the refinancing trap
Source: compiled from: https://www.nytimes.com/2008/11/12/business/12property.html; https://tucson.com/business/article_1ae04234-8585-5157-b010-5f0b8a87b378.html; https://wwd.com/business-news/financial/general-growth-shares-fall-on-missed-3rd-qtr-estimates-1851570/; https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/general-growth-properties-reports-wider-loss/
Date: 2008-11-13

The November 12 New York Times/AP report says the nation's second-largest mall owner warned of possible bankruptcy or debt default if it cannot refinance or extend nearly US$1 billion. The report describes a 64% share-price decline to approximately US$0.49 following the warning. The relevant fear is not merely an unpopular sector: common equity may have no recovery even if stores remain open.

The Arizona Daily Star's November 13 account quotes the company's warning: “Our potential inability to address our 2008 and 2009 debt maturities in a satisfactory fashion raises substantial doubts as to our ability to continue as a going concern.” The report identifies another US$3.07 billion of property/corporate debt due in 2009 on the consolidated basis. It quotes Citigroup analyst Michael Bilerman: “There is no quick fix in the current capital-constrained environment.”

The November 6 WWD story reports Banc of America analyst Christine McElroy's concern: “Actions taken by the new management team to reduce capital spending over the next few years, while necessary, may not be enough.” Management still expects about US$222 million development spending in Q4 according to that report. Avoiding unnecessary new projects cannot instantly eliminate completion commitments or principal maturities.

The Las Vegas Review-Journal's November 6 report quotes interim CEO Adam Metz: “Debt capital is an extraordinarily scarce resource.” RBC analyst Rich Moore complained that officials provided insufficient specificity about negotiations on individual properties. The lack of public transaction terms prevents an outside investor from treating announced strategic alternatives as funded solutions.

Two economically distinct bearish cases should be separated. In a liquidity crisis, sound assets cannot refinance on schedule and must be sold or reorganized. In an asset-value crisis, property earnings and market capitalization rates imply value below claims even if maturities are extended. Both can occur simultaneously.

The opposite observation is that the same November 5 company data show only a 0.5% decline in combined comparable mall/other NOI during Q3, not an operating collapse. But leases are not riskless: retailer bankruptcies can produce vacancies and lost recoveries. Any residual-equity argument therefore needs a cap-rate/NOI downside test, an actual legal mechanism and sufficient liquidity to survive the process, not simply the assertion that the malls are valuable.
