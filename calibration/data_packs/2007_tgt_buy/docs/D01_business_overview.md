Title: A design-led discount retailer with property and a card business
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/27419/000104746907001800/a2176656z10-k.htm
Date: 2007-03-15

Target operates a national US discount-store chain, combining everyday necessities with apparel, home décor and design-oriented merchandise under the “Expect More. Pay Less.” positioning. It competes on price, convenience, assortment and an aspirational shopping experience rather than attempting to be the lowest-price provider of every item.

Fiscal 2006 ended February 3, 2007 and contained 53 weeks. Total revenue was US$59.490 billion, comprising approximately US$57.878 billion merchandise sales and US$1.612 billion credit-card revenue. Calendar normalization matters when comparing growth against prior 52-week periods.

The chain had 1,488 stores: 1,260 classified as owned, 71 leased and 157 combination arrangements with owned buildings on leased land. Owned property supports operating control and lower explicit rent, but it also ties capital to sites and makes comparisons with more heavily leased retailers incomplete.

The balance sheet records land at US$4.934 billion and buildings/improvements at US$16.110 billion gross cost. Net property/equipment is US$21.431 billion after accumulated depreciation across property categories. These are accounting amounts, not independently appraised market values or immediately available cash. Retail earnings already benefit from using the properties.

The card operation provides financing to customers, supports loyalty and earns interest and fees. Gross year-end receivables were US$6.711 billion; the consolidated net receivable balance is US$6.194 billion. Card contribution to pretax earnings was US$693 million, a substantial component of consolidated earnings. Monetization would exchange an earning asset for cash rather than create the full proceeds as new equity value.

Chairman/CEO Bob Ulrich oversees a retailer with strong reported sales growth, improving operating profitability and continuing store expansion. CFO Doug Scovanner discusses both operating and financing matters. The contemporary record does not describe a broken retail operation requiring wholesale management replacement.

Merchandise risk remains meaningful. A material portion of sales comes from apparel, electronics and home categories exposed to fashion and discretionary spending. Competitive pricing, housing-related weakness, supply-chain execution, new-store returns and consumer credit can affect earnings together. The potential appeal is a differentiated franchise plus multiple asset pools; the analytical burden is to value each without double counting property income or ignoring costs of separation.
