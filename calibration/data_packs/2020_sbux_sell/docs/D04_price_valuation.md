Title: January price versus entry valuation and the company's outlook
Source: compiled from: https://fiscal.ai/company/NASDAQ_SBUX/; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/829224/000082922419000047/sbux-9292019xexhibit991.htm; https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2022/10/07150943/2018-Annual-Report-Letter-Only.pdf
Date: 2020-01-15

Historical daily data show Starbucks opening at $91.00 and closing at $91.54 on January 15, on volume of 6,145,518 shares. Only that historical row is used from the modern price database; its current company description and later prices are excluded.

At $91.54, the stock trades at 32.3x FY2019 non-GAAP EPS of $2.83 and 31.3x GAAP EPS of $2.92. Using the FY2020 adjusted guidance midpoint of $3.025 gives 30.3x.

The original owner letter said the purchase came at a 25% discount to a historical average of 26x forward earnings, implying roughly 19.5x on the manager's then-forward denominator. That was an entry-period judgment, not a constant intrinsic-value formula.

The present 30.3x company-guidance multiple and the historical 26x reference use different dates and potentially different forward conventions. Nonetheless, the source evidence clearly no longer describes the same discounted entry setup.

The $91.54 close is 78.4% above the manager's stated $51.30 average entry price before dividends. This is a simple price comparison, not the fund's realized return, trading result or a claim that every share was sold on the cutoff.

Using FY2019 ending shares of 1,184.6 million gives an indicative equity value of $108.435 billion. Adding long-term debt less cash of $8.480 billion gives approximately $116.916 billion before lease/other enterprise adjustments and any post-year-end share changes.

The same equity proxy against FY2019 CFO-minus-capex of $3.240 billion gives approximately a 3.0% cash-flow yield. This is a backward-looking calculation, not a normalized forecast; FY2018's transaction-heavy cash flow would be particularly misleading.

A verified current 52-week range, daily five-year valuation series, forward peer multiples and S&P multiple were not recovered. No cheap/expensive percentile is invented.

The valuation tension is explicit: a strong franchise with improving operations can still have less prospective return after substantial appreciation. An owner can recognize continuing business quality while independently reassessing the price, the EPS-growth outlook and the opportunity cost of committed capital.
