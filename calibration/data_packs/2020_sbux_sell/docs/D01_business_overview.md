Title: Global coffee franchise, owned stores and licensed distribution
Source: compiled from: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/829224/000082922419000047/sbux-9292019xexhibit991.htm; https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2022/10/07150943/2018-Annual-Report-Letter-Only.pdf
Date: 2019-10-30

Starbucks combines a global coffee brand, a company-operated store network, licensed stores and packaged/channel distribution. Its proposition includes coffee quality, convenience, customization, a familiar store experience and a rewards/digital relationship with frequent customers.

The company ended FY2019 with 31,256 stores, up 7% year over year. Store expansion and comparable-store activity are separate growth drivers: a mature location's traffic/check trends cannot be inferred from consolidated growth alone.

In the fourth quarter, company-operated store revenue was $5,480.1 million and licensed-store revenue $734.7 million. These business models have different recorded revenue and capital requirements. Licensed sales are not the full retail sales of the franchisee's store.

The global coffee alliance with Nestlé changes the channel-business mix and cash-flow interpretation. Transactional cash receipts and subsequent recognition should not be valued as if the same upfront proceeds recur every year.

US active rewards membership reached 17.6 million in Q4, up 15%. Rewards, mobile ordering, beverage innovation and convenience can reinforce customer frequency and retention. Their economic value depends on incremental profitable transactions rather than signups alone.

China remains a key growth market, with Q4 comparable sales up 5% and net store growth of 17% year over year. It also creates country, competition and execution exposure. Low historical per-capita consumption is an opportunity indicator, not a guaranteed market-share forecast.

CEO Kevin Johnson's team has focused on streamlining operations and prioritizing core growth. The company's October outlook reaffirms its long-term operating growth model. There is no pre-cutoff announcement in these documents of a fundamental abandonment of the coffee franchise or China growth strategy.

The moat thesis rests on habitual demand, brand, convenient locations and a broad consumer relationship. It is not immunity from wage inflation, competition, changes in consumer tastes or poorly chosen new sites.

The investor's question is therefore two-part: whether franchise quality remains intact, and whether the prospective per-share growth and cash distributions justify the current valuation. A well-run, growing company can offer a lower future return after a strong rerating without becoming an impaired business.
