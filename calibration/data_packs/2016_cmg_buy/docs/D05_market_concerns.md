Title: Bear case — customer trust, new safety costs and a damaged brand

Date: 2016-09-06

The bear case is an operating and reputational problem, not simply an expensive stock correcting. Food-safety incidents undermined the fresh-food promise at the center of the concept. The company can revise procedures, but the customer's willingness to return cannot be compelled.

The February 2 company release says the CDC investigation is over and welcomes customers back. Ending an outbreak investigation is not proof that brand perception or transaction frequency has normalized. The subsequent July results still show comparable sales down 23.6% and transactions down 19.3%.

The Wall Street Journal's September 6 coverage places the situation in the context of a beleaguered chain and activist attention. The pack omits the investor's disclosed transaction to avoid supplying a decision label. The dated financial evidence gives the substantive concern: an unusually large traffic decline at a model with company-owned restaurant costs.

The July release states supplier safety procedures and food waste increased food costs. Bears can reasonably argue that some portion of the old margin is not recoverable even if customers return. Additional testing, preparation changes and training can create a higher-cost operating model.

Promotions add another ambiguity. Chiptopia engagement and early July improvement may show renewed interest, but rewards can subsidize visits from customers who are already loyal. A campaign can increase transactions without restoring the same contribution per visit or long-term customer habit.

New restaurant openings continue during weak comparable sales. This can sustain system growth while straining cash or masking deteriorating mature-store economics. Strong old-unit returns do not guarantee that each incremental location earns the same return in a changed brand environment.

Repurchases also consume a finite reserve. First-half buybacks of $700.036 million exceed operating cash flow less capex by a wide margin. A no-debt balance sheet provides protection, but capital allocation during repair still matters.

The favorable alternative is that the customer proposition remains differentiated and trust can be rebuilt with demonstrably safe execution. The key uncertainty is permanence: temporary fear supports a recovery valuation, while a permanently lower frequency or cost structure requires a different earnings base. No later management appointment or subsequent sales recovery is used to answer that uncertainty.
