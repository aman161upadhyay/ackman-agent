The cleanest verified benchmark in the retrieved data is Chipotle's own pre-crisis operating base. It is not a substitute for a full peer valuation exercise, but it permits a direct analysis of how transactions affect a known company-owned model.

A restaurant-level margin excludes corporate overhead, depreciation and opening costs. Comparing Chipotle's 27%-28% restaurant contribution directly with a franchisor's consolidated operating margin is invalid. The franchisor records fees, while restaurant owners bear much of the sales and cost base.

Similarly, an owned-store company's capex and lease exposure differ from a franchise royalty platform. Restaurant openings can produce attractive incremental economics, but require buildings/improvements, equipment, training and working capital. A franchise growth multiple cannot be applied solely because both companies serve food quickly.

The industry competes on convenience, price, taste, perceived quality and consistency. Chipotle's focused menu and visible preparation can support throughput and a differentiated customer proposition. Those same attributes mean trust in ingredient handling and safety is economically important, rather than peripheral public relations.

A repaired unit base does not require a new customer proposition; it requires customers to resume using the existing one. That is analytically different from trying to transform a failing concept. However, evidence for recoverability must come from sustained traffic, repeat visits and cost performance, not simply past popularity.

Timestamped margins/growth/multiples for two to four restaurant peers were not independently recovered. The gap is explicit: this document provides internal operating benchmarks and comparability cautions, not an invented relative-value ranking. A final capital commitment should condition on a verified market price and a conservative normalized earnings model rather than rely on the old peak margin alone.
