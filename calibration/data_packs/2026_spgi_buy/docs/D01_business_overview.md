Title: Ratings, benchmarks, commodity intelligence and financial workflows

Date: 2026-03-18

S&P Global sells financial information, ratings, indices, commodity intelligence and mobility data. It is not a single desktop-software business. Different divisions earn revenue through subscriptions, transaction activity, benchmark licensing, asset-linked fees and other arrangements, with different exposure to issuance cycles and technology substitution.

Ratings benefits from established acceptance among issuers, investors and financial institutions. Its role depends on credibility, regulatory frameworks and market conventions. AI can assist analysis, but generating a plausible credit opinion is not the same as producing a rating recognized throughout the capital-markets ecosystem.

Indices licenses benchmark intellectual property used by investment products and market participants. Revenue can vary with assets, flows and trading, while benchmark adoption creates switching and coordination costs. Energy, including Platts, provides commodity information and reference assessments embedded in trading and commercial workflows.

Market Intelligence includes financial datasets and workflow tools. This division has more direct exposure to the concern that AI interfaces can replace research tasks or reduce demand for seats. That risk should be quantified separately from the value of licensed underlying data.

Mobility is a separate business slated for a mid-2026 separation. At this March cutoff the spin is a planned event, not a completed corporate action. Its revenues, costs and separation effects remain part of the current reporting framework.

Q4 revenue illustrates the mix: Market Intelligence $1.264 billion, Ratings $1.187 billion, Energy $576 million, Mobility $444 million and Indices $498 million, before intersegment eliminations. Operating profit was respectively $235 million, $723 million, $220 million, $71 million and $330 million. These are reported segment measures, not harmonized adjusted EBITDA.

Martina Cheung is president and CEO. Management must sustain trusted content, invest in delivery and AI integration, manage costs and allocate cash. Strong benchmarks do not eliminate debt-issuance cyclicality, fee pressure or data-licensing challenges.

The moat question is therefore specific: which products derive value from unique, trusted, legally usable information and collective adoption, and which derive value from an interface that could become cheaper or redundant? Treating every dollar of revenue as equally exposed would obscure the company's actual economic structure.
