Title: Bond shorts and payer options are different trades
Source: https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2023/03/29160536/Pershing-Square-Holdings-Ltd.-2022-Annual-Report.pdf
Date: 2023-03-29

The manager's already published 2022 annual report defines swaptions and discloses US and Japanese interest-rate option exposure. A swaption gives its owner a right, not an obligation, to enter or cancel a specified swap on a future date under agreed terms.

A payer swaption generally benefits when the relevant fixed swap rate rises relative to its strike. Its economics differ from selling a Treasury security outright. It has an option premium, expiry, underlying tenor and volatility exposure, not just linear bond duration.

An outright short bond position gains when bond prices fall but can suffer losses from a rally, financing/borrow costs and coupon payments. Losses are not inherently capped at an initial option premium.

A purchased option can define the upfront premium at risk, subject to its actual terms and any separate financing arrangements. That potential convexity is attractive for insurance, but time decay, expensive implied volatility and wrong maturity/strike can make a correct macro view unprofitable.

Swap rates and Treasury yields can diverge. A payer swaption on a long swap is not identical to an option on a particular Treasury bond. The hedge therefore includes basis risk alongside the broad interest-rate direction.

The annual report's year-end risk table gives a US thirty-year option fair value of approximately $330.722 million. That is a historical mark, not original cost, notional, premium budget or a verified August 2 valuation.

The report's rate-shock sensitivities demonstrate nonlinearity, but they are model-based year-end disclosures. They cannot be treated as August dealer quotes or a guarantee that the same gains/losses occur after time and market conditions change.

No current contract-level strike, expiry, notional, premium or implied volatility was recovered. The pack therefore cannot establish a precise five-to-tenfold payoff or a specific percent-NAV cost. An analyst should request those terms rather than infer them from a yield forecast.

The decision is not merely “will long yields rise?” It is whether the chosen structure pays usefully in the portfolio's adverse scenario, has an affordable carry and can be monetized when its expected protection value changes. Existing gains, if any, are capital at risk too.
