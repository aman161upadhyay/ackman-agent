Title: The long curve at the cutoff
Source: compiled from: https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/DGS10; https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/DGS30; https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20230726a.htm
Date: 2023-08-02

Federal Reserve H.15 constant-maturity data show a 10-year Treasury yield of 4.08% and 30-year yield of 4.17% on August 2. These are daily benchmark yield observations, not the execution price or strike of a particular derivative.

The 30-year series was 4.02% on July 31 and 4.11% on August 1. It had already risen 15bp over those two sessions by the cutoff. The pack uses only historical rows through August 2 from the modern database and excludes later observations.

The policy target range is 5.25%-5.50%, above both long-maturity yields. The curve therefore does not simply project the current policy rate unchanged for thirty years. Expected normalization and term compensation matter.

The 30-year nominal yield is not a market inflation forecast. It combines real yields, expected inflation and risk/term premia. No dated TIPS breakeven series or model term-premium estimate was independently recovered, so the pack does not invent a precise decomposition.

A long-duration bond has substantial price sensitivity to yield changes. The sensitivity varies by coupon, maturity and price; constant-maturity yield data do not identify a single bond's exact duration or convexity.

Likewise, payer-swaption pricing depends on the underlying swap rate, strike, expiry, tenor and implied volatility. A Treasury benchmark yield is relevant context but not a complete option-price input, and swap/Treasury basis can move.

An exact executable options surface, premium, notional, dealer bid/ask and funding/collateral terms were not recovered. There is no numeric claim that an unspecified structure offers a particular payoff multiple or limited premium percentage.

The observed yield provides a starting point for scenario analysis: further inflation/rate repricing could hurt long bonds and equity duration, while disinflation or recession could produce a rally. The trade's attractiveness depends on the distribution of those moves relative to the insurance price.

The current rate is neither the much lower pre-tightening level nor a later peak. Assessing expected return requires acknowledging that material repricing has already occurred. Historical cheap-insurance successes do not prove that today's protection is equally cheap.
