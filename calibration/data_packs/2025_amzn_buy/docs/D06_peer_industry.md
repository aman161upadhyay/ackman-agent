Title: Cloud peers and why retail requires a different benchmark
Source: compiled from: https://www.cnbc.com/2025/01/29/microsoft-msft-q2-earnings-report-2025.html; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000101872425000002/amzn-20241231xex991.htm; https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/06/amazon-expects-to-spend-100-billion-on-capital-expenditures-in-2025.html
Date: 2025-02-07

Amazon competes in two different industry structures. In cloud infrastructure, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud are the most relevant large-platform alternatives. In US retail, Walmart and other broad retailers compete for household spending, while third-party merchants can also use competing marketplaces or their own stores. A single consolidated peer P/E can conceal these differences.

For the December 2024 quarter, AWS reported 19% revenue growth to $28.8 billion and $10.6 billion operating profit. Its calculated operating margin is approximately 36.8%. Microsoft's January 29 results, reported by CNBC, show Azure and other cloud-services revenue growth of 31%, down from 33% in the preceding quarter. Microsoft's broader Intelligent Cloud segment produced $25.54 billion revenue, up approximately 19%. Azure's percentage growth is not directly comparable with the broader segment's dollar sales.

| Published benchmark | Observation | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| AWS, Q4 2024 | 19% sales growth; approximately 36.8% operating margin | Standalone reported segment |
| Azure and other cloud services, December quarter | 31% growth | Different size and product scope |
| Amazon North America, Q4 | approximately 8.0% operating margin | Includes advertising and seller services |
| Amazon International, Q4 | approximately 3.0% operating margin | Geography and investment maturity differ |

Google Cloud is a relevant competitor, but a verified matching quarter's figures and pre-cutoff multiple comparison were not recovered for this pack. The same applies to Walmart's tariff-adjusted margins. No present-day peer data are substituted.

The industry-wide capex race cuts both ways. Scale, custom chips, distribution and existing customer relationships can support defensibility. Conversely, competing hyperscalers can add capacity simultaneously, increasing the risk of price competition or low returns if monetization disappoints.

Retail logistics density is a different moat: delivery speed and unit cost improve when orders share facilities and routes. Unlike a software network, this requires substantial physical investment. The underwriting task is to determine whether that installed network creates incremental margin opportunity, while treating growth capex and operating expenses consistently across competitors.
