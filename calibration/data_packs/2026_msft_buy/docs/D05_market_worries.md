Title: Sell-side debate — Azure execution and whether Copilot earns its compute
Source: compiled from: https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/29/microsoft-market-cap-earnings.html; https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/microsoft-earnings-good-quarter-with-ai-demand-ramping-azure-good; https://fortune.com/2026/01/28/microsoft-stock-drops-azure-growth-slows-capex-spending-q2/
Date: 2026-01-29

Jordan Novet's January 29 CNBC report gives a precise explanation for the negative reaction. Azure grew 39%, below StreetAccount's 39.4% consensus. The next-quarter More Personal Computing revenue outlook of about $12.6 billion was below $13.7 billion consensus, and implied operating margin also disappointed. Thus the selloff was not caused by reported revenue declining across the whole company.

The more important strategic question is capital allocation. CNBC reports that Microsoft prioritized internal AI applications over allocating all new compute to external Azure customers. UBS analysts led by Karl Keirstead wrote: “M365 revs growth is not accelerating due to Copilot” and said many customer checks did not show a strong usage ramp. They concluded: “We think Microsoft needs to 'prove' that these are good investments.” These are dated analyst observations, not established product-failure facts.

Melius Research's Ben Reitzes, still with a buy rating, offered a different criticism: “there's an execution issue here with Azure, where they need to literally stand up buildings a little faster.” This argues for more effective capacity delivery, not less demand. The two criticisms can coexist: poor allocation or slow construction can prevent strong customer demand from becoming attractive shareholder returns.

Bernstein analysts led by Mark Moerdler defended management's conscious decision to focus on the company's long-term interests rather than maximizing the current quarter's share price. Morningstar's Dan Romanoff, January 29, maintained a $600 fair-value estimate and highlighted broad cloud demand. He noted RPO rose 110%, but 28% excluding OpenAI. The comparison is important because backlog concentration can make an aggregate growth figure look stronger than the diversified customer base.

Fortune's January 28 account, published around the earnings release, juxtaposes slower Azure growth with elevated spending and continuing capacity constraints. The underlying bear mechanism is that revenue growth, current margins and the service lives of expensive equipment may not support adequate returns on the expanded capital base.

Additional analytical risks include AI reducing software seats, alternative models capturing value instead of the platform, and OpenAI financing obligations becoming harder to fulfill. None is a demonstrated outcome at the cutoff. Conversely, paid Copilot seats and growing cloud demand do not alone prove successful incremental monetization. The decision requires separating a durable distribution franchise from potentially lower-return new investment.
