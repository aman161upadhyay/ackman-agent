Title: Essential mortgage infrastructure with an impaired common security
Source: compiled from: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/310522/000031052213000065/fanniemae201210k.htm; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/310522/000031052213000138/a2013q2pressrelease.htm
Date: 2013-08-08

Fannie Mae purchases and guarantees residential mortgages, issuing mortgage-backed securities that investors can trade without individually underwriting every borrower. It supports purchase, refinance and rental housing finance. Freddie Mac performs a broadly comparable role under a separate charter and capital structure.

Scale, lender relationships, underwriting data, servicing infrastructure and a liquid securities market are difficult to replicate quickly. These enterprises are important to the availability of long-term fixed-rate mortgages. That does not establish that their present common shareholders own an unrestricted claim to the resulting profits.

Fannie has operated in FHFA conservatorship since September2008. Treasury provides a contractual funding backstop in exchange for senior preferred interests and warrants. Management describes the company as managed in taxpayers' overall interest, not as an ordinary shareholder-controlled corporation. The government can pursue policy goals that conflict with common investors' returns.

The business has single-family, multifamily and capital-markets activities. Guarantee economics depend on fees, credit losses, operating costs and required capital; retained mortgage investments also have interest-rate and funding risk. Retained portfolios are subject to mandated reduction, so historical investment earnings are not automatically a sustainable growth business.

Fannie's June2013 single-family book is approximately US$2.84 trillion. A very small change in credit losses on a book of this scale matters. Newer underwriting and recovering home prices have improved credit quality, but the old crisis-vintage book still contributes losses and reserve uncertainty.

The annual report identifies higher guaranty fees on new acquisitions as a contributor to future profitability. A scaled franchise with repriced new business could have substantial economic value if privately capitalized. The missing bridge is the allocation of that value among Treasury, junior preferreds, new capital and today's common shares.

A common investment therefore contains at least two separate judgments: the franchise can continue to produce attractive earnings, and legal/political decisions will eventually allow existing common equity to participate. Strong operating results are necessary evidence for the first proposition, not sufficient proof of the second.

An activist cannot simply replace the CEO, win a proxy contest and stop the Treasury dividend. Conservatorship, statutory powers and the purchase agreements dominate the normal corporate-governance toolkit.
