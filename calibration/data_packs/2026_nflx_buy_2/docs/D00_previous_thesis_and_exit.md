Title: The 2022 underwriting and why it was withdrawn
Source: compiled from: https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2022/01/26170421/Pershing-Square-Capital-Management-L.P.-Releases-Letter-to-Investors-01-26-2022.pdf; https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2022/04/20184527/Letter-to-Shareholders-4.20.2022.pdf
Date: 2022-04-20

The January 26, 2022 investor letter disclosed more than 3.1 million Netflix shares purchased beginning the preceding Friday. That is a pre-November-2025-split share count and is not comparable directly to today's shares. The underwriting emphasized streaming replacing linear television, subscription recurrence, content scale, pricing, margins and future free cash flow.

Short verbatim purchase-thesis extracts describe “subscription-based, highly recurring revenues,” “a truly best-in-class management team” and “pricing power derived from the enormous value it delivers to consumers compared with other alternatives.” Economies of scale were expected to spread content investment over a larger paying audience. Improving cash conversion was part of the case rather than a separate afterthought.

The April 20, 2022 exit letter confirms the entire investment was sold. The loss reduced funds' year-to-date returns by four percentage points. The letter did not say Netflix could never succeed. It explained that operating leverage makes intrinsic value particularly sensitive to subscriber-growth assumptions, and that recent disappointments plus a changed business model expanded forecast uncertainty.

Its explicit standard was: “We require a high degree of predictability in the businesses in which we invest due to the highly concentrated nature of our portfolio.” Management's plans to monetize account sharing and introduce advertising might be sensible, but Pershing could not confidently forecast their impact on future subscribers, revenue, margins or capital intensity.

The letter says it had “lost confidence in our ability to predict the company’s future prospects with a sufficient degree of certainty.” The issue was underwriting dispersion, not a claim that every original competitive advantage had vanished. It also emphasizes promptly acting on information inconsistent with an investment thesis.

Any fresh assessment must therefore reconcile two questions. Have advertising, paid sharing, content economics and cash conversion become sufficiently observable to restore forecast confidence? And does the price compensate for whatever uncertainty remains? A lower stock price alone cannot answer the first question, while subsequent business improvement alone cannot answer the second. The historical loss is relevant evidence about the required predictability standard, not an instruction either to avoid the company forever or to repeat the original position.
