Title: What was known about international contagion on February 24
Source: compiled from: https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/coronaviruse/situation-reports/20200224-sitrep-35-covid-19.pdf; https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/84735/cdc_84735_DS1.pdf
Date: 2020-02-24

WHO Situation Report 35 gives data reported by 10am CET on February 24. Globally, 79,331 confirmed cases were reported, including 77,262 in China and 2,069 outside China. Outside-China cases were spread across 29 countries, with 300 new confirmed cases in the preceding reporting interval.

WHO reported 2,595 deaths in China and 23 outside China. The risk assessment was very high for China and high at regional and global levels. These are reported surveillance data, not an estimate of all infections or a prediction of eventual economic damage.

The January 30 CDC telebriefing had already announced person-to-person transmission in the United States between close household contacts. CDC also emphasized low risk to the general public in that setting. Both facts belong in the evidence: transmission was established, while authorities had not characterized all US communities as experiencing widespread uncontrolled infection.

By February 24, international clusters created a problem not confined to Chinese factory shutdowns. Restrictions, voluntary changes in behavior and uncertainty could affect travel, consumer activity and supply chains in multiple economies at once.

The investment question is not whether every eventual shutdown can be predicted. It is whether a sufficiently plausible severe interruption has a market price inconsistent with its potential impact on a concentrated portfolio.

Confirmed-case counts have reporting lags, testing limitations and differences across jurisdictions. A precise epidemiological model is not supplied. The documents do not infer an eventual infection fatality rate from the ratio of current deaths to current reported cases.

Neither a March pandemic declaration nor subsequent US lockdowns, central-bank programs, market lows or portfolio gains is included. The cutoff captures an emerging international threat with meaningful uncertainty, not a crisis whose full path is already known.

The favorable scenario remains containment, improved treatment/behavior and a limited interruption. The adverse scenario is a self-reinforcing contraction in mobility and spending accompanied by tighter financial conditions. A hedge decision must compare those possibilities with the cost, timing and basis risk of the proposed protection rather than assume the severe scenario is certain.
