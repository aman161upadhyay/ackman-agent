Title: Comparing credit protection, equity puts and reducing exposure
Source: compiled from: https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt1806b.htm; https://www.cnbc.com/2020/02/24/us-futures-coronavirus-outbreak.html; https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/2020/02/03182429/Performance-Report-January-2020-PSH.pdf
Date: 2020-02-24

The main alternatives to credit-index protection are selling long positions, holding more cash, buying equity puts, reducing leverage or accepting the risk. They address different dimensions of the problem and should not be treated as economically identical.

Selling equities immediately removes exposure and raises cash without a recurring insurance premium. It may also crystallize taxes, forgo recoveries and require rebuilding concentrated stakes later. Those trade-offs depend on cost bases, valuation and portfolio constraints, which are not fully documented here.

Equity index puts more directly target an equity market decline but introduce strike, expiry and volatility pricing. February 24's large VIX move indicates that equity insurance pricing has changed; it does not quantify a particular put's cost or prove credit is cheaper on a risk-adjusted basis.

Credit-index protection offers a different stress exposure: wider spreads, defaults and increased credit risk can raise its value. It can be monetized before actual default if counterparties and market liquidity permit. The basis to the manager's actual equities remains material.

Reducing leverage can improve resilience without a derivative, but funding liabilities, asset sales and vehicle-specific capital arrangements matter. The January public NAV figures alone do not establish the exact leverage or debt-repayment opportunities available on February 24.

Doing nothing preserves full upside and avoids premium drag. That can be rational if risk is fairly priced or the portfolio already has adequate liquidity. It is less attractive if a small defined carrying cost can offset a plausible severe loss and create scarce reinvestment capital.

A proper comparison needs a common scenario set: contained outbreak, prolonged modest slowdown, synchronized recession and sharp financial disruption. For each, estimate equity losses, hedge gains/losses, cash flows, collateral and the time until payment.

No authentic dealer quotes for those alternatives were recovered, so no numeric “best hedge” ranking is supplied. The document sets out the decision dimensions, not a fabricated backtest or an outcome-based proof.

The central discipline is to buy exposure whose downside is affordable and whose stressed payoff is relevant. A convincing epidemic narrative is insufficient if the insurance is already expensive, the maturity wrong or the portfolio lacks the liquidity to carry it.
