## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
At the late-2010 public entry disclosure, J.C. Penney was an established department-store retailer. Pershing disclosed 16.5%; the later Ron Johnson plan was **not yet an accomplished turnaround**. A complete contemporaneous entry financial/real-estate valuation and original investment deck remain missing. The initial thesis must not be reduced to later evidence that customers rejected everyday-low pricing. [5]

Fortune's January 24, 2011 contemporary account dates first buying to **mid-August 2010**, when shares were at a two-year low below $20; it describes an initial approximately $900 million/39 million-share commitment at approximately $23 average. These are initial-accumulation figures, not necessarily the final 2013 all-in cost basis. [7]

## Ackman's thesis in his own words
- **Original-entry quote gap:** no authenticated 2010 pitch/deck recovered. Recruiting Johnson and backing a transformation are documented actions, but not substitutes for a verbatim original valuation case. [2,4]
- **Contemporaneous reassessment, April 2013:** “too much change too quickly without adequate testing on what the impact would be” — Ackman's assessment reported by Forbes after Johnson's removal; this is **hindsight on the turnaround plan**, not the 2010 entry thesis. [2]
- **August 9, 2013 board letter:** “I think JC Penney is at a very critical stage in its history and its very existence is at risk.” The letter also questioned relationships outside JCP affecting board decisions. This is a crisis/succession thesis after the operating failure, not evidence the original investment was a distressed-equity rescue. [1]

## What Pershing Square did
| Date | Action / information |
|---|---|
| October 2010 | Disclosed 16.5% stake. [5] |
| 2011 | Helped recruit former Apple retail executive Ron Johnson as CEO; Ackman served on the board. [2,4] |
| 2012 | Backed a transformation involving removal of the established coupon/promotional model and redesigned stores/merchandising. [2,4] |
| April 2013 | Johnson was removed and Mike Ullman returned. [2,4] |
| August 8–9, 2013 | Public letters sought faster CEO succession and a change of chairman from Thomas Engibous; the board resisted. [1,5] |
| August 2013 | Resigned from board; sold approximately 39.1 million shares, nearly 18%, in an offering expected to raise approximately $504.4 million. Proceeds are not profit. [3–5] |

## What happened after
Johnson's pricing/format reset did not restore the franchise: fiscal-year sales fell approximately 25%, depriving the company of about $4.3 billion of revenue in the year ended February 2, 2013. This outcome belongs here, not in the entry situation. [2]

Fortune's March 2014 reconstruction puts Ackman's loss at approximately $470 million; the task's “~$500M” is a reasonable rounding, not a second independent loss estimate. The August 2013 sale proceeds of approximately $504 million are **not** the loss. [3–4]

Ackman's April 2013 criticism of speed and inadequate testing is a contemporaneous post-mortem. A complete signed Pershing investor-letter post-mortem was **not recovered**; do not turn the newspaper reconstruction into his own words. August 21 reporting also records his admission of retail-investment mistakes. [2,6]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Underperforming franchise:** transformation sought improvement rather than liquidation; original numerical upside model unverified. [2,4]
- **Exceptional leadership:** Johnson's Apple reputation mattered to recruitment; a successful executive elsewhere was not proof the customer transition would work. [2,4]
- **Customer proposition:** everyday-low pricing and store changes were substantive operating assumptions, not financial engineering. [2,4]
- **Board accountability:** August letters specifically challenged succession speed and chairman leadership. [1]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: high influence and management change fit activism, but the need to **change customer behavior**, execute a rapid retailer reset and maintain board cooperation made the business less predictable. The documented lesson is testing and pace, not a verified blanket rule that Ackman would never invest in retail again. [1–2,4]

## Public discussion
Forbes' April 9 article debated whether the strategy itself or execution was wrong. The August board letter, company resistance and Ackman's resignation were covered in real time by Business Insider and the Los Angeles Times. Fortune's March 2014 account is retrospective and includes competing director/management perspectives. No verified contemporaneous VIC, COBF, Reddit or Twitter thread was recovered. [1–2,4–5]
