# Recurring reasoning patterns: decision templates with counterexamples

**As of 2026-10-25.** These **20 patterns are analyst synthesis**, not twenty rules Ackman enumerated. They overlap: a trade can be a quality compounder, management repair and valuation dislocation simultaneously. Each example is dated to the *source available for its explanation*, not silently backdated to entry. Sources are below. Exact position sizes are **unknown unless stated**; “core” means potentially a meaningful concentrated holding, not an invented fixed allocation.

**Common sizing discipline:** the formal mandate is usually 8–12 core holdings; GD15 describes sizing upside against downside, with Fannie/Freddie much smaller than Allergan. Neither 10% nor 20% is an automatic recommendation. A26's fifteen issuer rows also show that the typical mandate is not an absolute count limit. General exits are intrinsic-value/return-hurdle convergence, a better use of capital, excessive concentration, or reduced thesis predictability. [GD15; A21; A22; H126]

## 1. Great business, poor operator → recruit proven leadership

- **Trigger/evidence:** a durable franchise materially underperforms comparable operators; isolate fixable execution, incentives and capital allocation rather than blame irreparable industry economics. Compare operating ratios/margins and confirm the replacement has relevant experience.
- **Valuation:** earnings/FCF under plausible optimized operations, with a defensible “as-is” case; **sizing:** core if downside and implementation are tolerable, no pattern-specific fixed weight; **catalyst:** board/CEO change and measurable operating milestones; **exit:** optimized earnings recognized, better opportunity or execution/governance failure.
- **Examples:** **CP** (November 2011 letter: unresolved peer-performance gap; later Harrison appointment); **Air Products** (2014 annual: Ghasemi and margin improvement); **Lowe's** (H1 2018: Home Depot gap and Ellison). **Counterexample:** **JCP** shows prestigious leadership is not equivalent to applicable turnaround skill. [Q311; A14; H118; Q313]

## 2. Hidden assets / incompatible businesses → separation or sum-of-parts realization

- **Trigger/evidence:** one investor base or reporting perimeter obscures distinct earnings/assets; examine segment cash flows, separation feasibility, capital structure and taxes.
- **Valuation:** independently valued components less debt, separation cost and stranded overhead; **sizing:** core only when underlying downside is supported; **catalyst:** spin, asset sale, strategic review or bankruptcy plan; **exit:** realized separation value or higher-return alternative—not necessarily immediately on spin day.
- **Examples:** **Wendy's/Tim Hortons** (2005 campaign discussed in A14); **Fortune Brands/Beam/FBHS** (November 2011 letter); **GGP/HHC** (2014 retrospective of 2010 creation); **APD/Versum** (2016 annual). A14 explicitly says the HHC creation was not fully envisioned at the original GGP entry. [A14; Q311; A16]

## 3. Temporary operating/brand crisis in a durable franchise → buy only if franchise survives

- **Trigger/evidence:** price collapses after a discrete shock; investigate unit economics, customer behavior, recovery path, liquidity and whether apparent “temporary” trouble has structurally impaired demand.
- **Valuation:** normalized future FCF with costs/time to recover; **sizing:** core or smaller repair allocation depending on impairment risk, never mechanically increase because price is down; **catalyst:** operational recovery, leadership change, customer return; **exit:** recovery priced, capital better deployed, or evidence the franchise was not durable.
- **Examples:** **Chipotle** (2016 annual food-safety recovery); **Nike** (2024 annual new-CEO turnaround); **Hertz** (H1 2025 operating/fleet repair). **Important distinction:** the Nike and Hertz cases are not “business completely fine” examples; meaningful operational change was required. [A16; A24; H125]

## 4. Narrative panic in an intact quality business → valuation/watchlist opportunity

- **Trigger/evidence:** a long-followed company becomes affordable because the market extrapolates near-term news; test the contested causal mechanism rather than just the price chart.
- **Valuation:** recurring earnings/DCF and prospective IRR, including realistic growth investment; **sizing:** potentially core after diligence; **catalyst:** ordinary execution disproves narrative, no activist event required; **exit:** price overtakes prospective return or thesis durability declines.
- **Examples:** **Alphabet 2023** (AI search fear, A23); **Amazon April 2025** (tariff-driven market volatility, H125/A25); **Meta late 2025** (AI-capex concern and watchlist entry, A25); **Netflix 2026** (re-established predictability plus attractive repricing, H126/KP26). Do **not** use the successful 2026 Netflix case to rewrite what was knowable in 2022. [A23; H125; A25; H126; KP26]

## 5. Royalty-like / asset-light growth → compound fee streams rather than own all physical capital

- **Trigger/evidence:** network, brand, contracts or content rights permit revenue growth without proportional corporate capex; inspect franchisee/customer economics, retention, pricing, and who actually funds growth.
- **Valuation:** incremental FCF, unit growth, fees/royalties, operating leverage and buybacks; **sizing:** core when durability and valuation pass; **catalyst:** ordinary unit/customer expansion and capital return; **exit:** inadequate future IRR, moat deterioration or broken underlying counterparties.
- **Examples:** **Restaurant Brands** (2015/2016 letters' capital-light franchise model); **Hilton** (2019 initiation narrative and February 2026 exit); **UMG** (2021–22 long-term content/rights ownership); **Visa/Mastercard** (2026 networks). Asset-light is not risk-free: Hilton still suffered a severe travel-demand shock. [A15; A16; A19; A21; A25; H126]

## 6. Asymmetric macro view → cheap option-like instrument, not an open-ended directional gamble

- **Trigger/evidence:** an important risk is underpriced; must independently establish both variant event probability and attractive instrument pricing. Specify premium, notional, maturity, basis/counterparty risk and monetization conditions.
- **Valuation:** probability-weighted payoff against premium/capital at risk; **sizing:** modest loss budget, large notional can be misleading; **catalyst:** event likelihood or market pricing changes; **exit:** thesis recognized, payoff/hedge economics less attractive, or changed macro risk.
- **Examples:** **2020 pandemic CDS**; **2021–22 rate swaptions**; **2023 Treasury-duration short**. **Counterevidence:** A23's overall hedging program lost 187bp in 2023; not every asymmetric position succeeded. Cheap insurance is not an obligation to buy insurance at any price. [A19; A21; A23; DA23]

## 7. Crisis liquidity → monetize protection and buy more durable equities

- **Trigger/evidence:** hedge gains become liquid while long-term business prices disconnect from value; inspect solvency and survival, not merely historical highs.
- **Valuation:** re-estimate long-run distributable cash after crisis; **sizing:** governed by current portfolio loss/concentration budget; **catalyst:** policy/recovery and underlying business resilience; **exit:** equity recovery or a later fundamental/valuation decision.
- **Examples:** March 2020 additions to **Hilton**, **Lowe's**, **Restaurant Brands**, and **Howard Hughes** funded alongside the CDS unwind; A19 lists increases in these holdings. These are **four equity redeployments from one hedge episode**, not four independent macro calls. [A19]

## 8. Regulatory/political optionality → scenarios and a possible-total-loss budget

- **Trigger/evidence:** essential underlying assets plus a legal, regulatory or government-capital-structure constraint; distinguish legal entitlement, policy intention and enforceable implementation.
- **Valuation:** scenario-weighted recoveries with dilution/recapitalization and time-to-resolution; **sizing:** explicitly smaller in GD15 because complete loss was possible; **catalyst:** litigation, capital rules, recapitalization or conservatorship exit; **exit:** adequate scenario value realized, scenario probabilities worsen, or another use of risk capital dominates.
- **Examples:** **Fannie Mae common**, **Freddie Mac common**, and **preferred shares added as a restructuring hedge** in the 2017 letter. These are closely related securities, **not independent evidence of three unrelated wins**. H125/H126 update privatization expectations; expectations are not completed government actions. [GD15; A17; H125; H126]

## 9. AI/automation changes the profit pool → identify who captures value

- **Trigger/evidence:** consensus labels an incumbent a technology loser; test distribution, proprietary data, customer acquisition, network density, monetization and capital required by alternative providers.
- **Valuation:** cash generation after transition, not an arbitrary “AI premium”; **sizing:** core only if outcomes remain predictable, smaller if optionality dominates; **catalyst:** product/partnership/earnings evidence; **exit:** captured economics fail or price discounts too much success.
- **Examples:** **Alphabet** (search/distribution/cloud); **Uber** (AV suppliers may need its demand network); **Amazon** (AWS and AI demand); **Hertz** (AV/fleet-related optionality, not a proven stand-alone cash-flow outcome). A25/H126 support the first three in detail; H125 contains the Hertz operating thesis, so **do not let AV optionality replace its basic turnaround economics**. KP26 also emphasizes that AI raises disruption risk, not only upside. [A23; H125; A25; H126; KP26]

## 10. Build a permanent-capital platform → remove the forced-exit constraint

- **Trigger/evidence:** a productive investment strategy is constrained by investor liquidity or lacks a public permanent vehicle; analyze liabilities, fee incentives, governance, market discounts and shareholder alignment.
- **Valuation:** underlying portfolio NAV/compounding plus vehicle-specific debt/fees; manager fee earnings are a different cash-flow stream; **sizing:** vehicle/control-level decision, not standard stock weight; **catalyst:** listing, strategic capital, new mandate; **exit:** no preset sale necessary, but per-share value and economics must justify continued ownership.
- **Examples:** **PSH 2014 listing/2018 capital reset**; **PSUS 2024 proposal and 2026 IPO**; **HHH 2025–26 holding-company transformation**; **Pershing Square Inc. 2026 manager IPO**. The abandoned 2024 PSUS structure is evidence that investor/aftermarket economics can defeat an initially proposed platform. [A14; A21; A24; P26; PSI26]

## 11. Control-like influence without paying a control premium

- **Trigger/evidence:** minority ownership and credible expertise can improve outcomes; determine actual board access, other shareholder support, legal rights and management receptivity.
- **Valuation:** independent franchise value plus achievable improvements, not assumed total command; **sizing:** a sufficiently meaningful stake, constrained by liquidity/loss budget; **catalyst:** negotiated directors or leadership/strategy agreement; **exit:** change achieved, diminished influence, alignment breaks or valuation closes.
- **Examples:** **CP**, **Air Products**, **ADP**. H119 compares the strategy with private equity but distinguishes avoiding auction control premiums; ADP's failed proxy contest proves influence is not guaranteed. [Q311; A14; A17; H117; H119]

## 12. Best management already in place → be a patient passive/constructive owner

- **Trigger/evidence:** business, leadership and capital allocation already meet requirements; do not manufacture a campaign merely to resemble an activist.
- **Valuation:** long-run cash/earnings growth at attractive entry price; **sizing:** core possible even without board seat; **catalyst:** execution, not compulsory corporate disruption; **exit:** return hurdle, governance deterioration or superior investment.
- **Examples:** **Restaurant Brands/3G** (A14 specifically says no Pershing board seat needed); **Agilent** (2020 presentation/A19); **Hilton** (A25 praises Nassetta at exit); **Berkshire 2019** (A19). “Passive” describes absence of an activist campaign, not absence of diligence. [A14; A19; A25]

## 13. Replace GAAP with economic earnings → but audit the normalization

- **Trigger/evidence:** accounting obscures recurring cash or capital obligations; reconcile D&A, maintenance spending, working capital, acquisition adjustments, stock compensation and true reinvestment.
- **Valuation:** normalized owner earnings, with growth and capital consumed considered together; **sizing:** cannot be increased just because an adjusted metric looks cheap; **catalyst:** improving disclosure or realized cash; **exit:** normalization disproved or price catches up.
- **Examples:** **Valeant** (GD15's accounting discussion, later catastrophic challenge); **Citigroup 2010** (tangible book/deferred-tax/excess-capital analysis); **Amazon 2025–26** (growth versus maintenance capex); **HHH** (underlying development/asset cash not captured by a simplistic earnings multiple). The Valeant example is a **failure mode**, not evidence that reported accounting can safely be ignored. [GD15; Q110; A16; A25; H126]

## 14. Company buys its own discounted stock → per-share compounding accelerates

- **Trigger/evidence:** durable cash flow exceeds productive reinvestment needs and stock trades below value; verify actual shares retired, funding, dilution and debt.
- **Valuation:** FCF per share after economically accretive repurchases; **sizing:** core if franchise and entry price pass; **catalyst:** sustained repurchases, not just authorization; **exit:** valuation reduces repurchase/holding return or buybacks undermine solvency.
- **Examples:** **Hilton** (A25 says more than 20% of shares retired during holding period); **Lowe's** (A18/H120 ongoing execution and capital return); **PSH's own shares** (A23/A24 discount and buybacks). PSH buybacks can enlarge concentration in remaining holdings; A18 discusses this trade-off. [A18; H120; A23; A24; A25]

## 15. Price appreciation reduces prospective return → sell even a wonderful business

- **Trigger/evidence:** the market recognizes the original opportunity or multiple expansion outruns future earnings; compare future return with alternatives, not cost basis or emotional attachment.
- **Valuation:** updated IRR from **today's** price; **sizing:** trim or fully exit as appropriate; **catalyst:** no corporate event required; **exit:** this pattern is the exit, with re-entry permitted later.
- **Examples:** **YUM Q2 2010** (approached intrinsic value); **Nike 2017** (34% gain in four months reduced expected return); **Starbucks January 2020** (intrinsic value, later repurchased); **Hilton February 2026** (about 32x earnings and hurdle failure). Price appreciation alone is not a sell signal if intrinsic value compounds sufficiently. [Q210; A17; A19; A25]

## 16. New information destroys confidence → sell before complete certainty of failure

- **Trigger/evidence:** forecast dispersion widens, management changes fundamental strategy, or governance prevents execution; distinguish loss of analytical certainty from proof the company will fail.
- **Valuation:** wider downside scenarios invalidate a high-conviction concentrated position; **sizing:** reduce/exit rather than preserve an arbitrary original allocation; **catalyst:** new report, management disclosure, governance conflict; **exit:** prompt redeployment, no break-even requirement.
- **Examples:** **Netflix April 2022** (subscriber/strategy uncertainty); **JCP October 2013 account** (board alignment and turnaround); **Valeant March 2017** (continuing demands and reassessed risk). Valeant additionally fits attention-cost pattern below; do not conflate all three explanations. [Q313; A16; A22; KP26]

## 17. An attractive rebound is not enough → optimize capital and management attention

- **Trigger/evidence:** a troubled position occupies disproportionate time but is too small to move portfolio returns; or acceptable expected return trails a better available use of capital.
- **Valuation:** portfolio-level risk-adjusted opportunity cost, including scarce research/engagement resources; **sizing:** remaining weight and meaningful contribution matter; **catalyst:** replacement opportunity or completed stabilization; **exit:** accept loss and free attention.
- **Examples:** **Valeant 2017** (about 3% remaining weight); **Herbalife 2018** (exit later defended alongside Valeant in A21); **Berkshire 2020** (redeployment rather than denunciation of quality). “Do not need to make it back the way you lost it” is a retrospective A21 principle, not evidence of the 2015 entry thesis. [A16; A18; H120; A21]

## 18. Re-underwrite extrinsic risk → sell a good business if risk composition worsens

- **Trigger/evidence:** tariffs, economic cycle or regulation becomes more influential relative to the rest of the portfolio; distinguish predictable operating durability from uncertain external shocks.
- **Valuation:** stress adjusted earnings and downside, then compare alternatives; **sizing:** trim/exit or segregate a smaller optionality position; **catalyst:** external policy/regime change; **exit:** risk-adjusted return falls even if franchise quality remains high.
- **Examples:** **CP April 2025** (tariff sensitivity); **Lowe's 2023** (less certain prospective returns in macro environment); **Valeant postmortem** (political/regulatory factors and excessive size). These are **three differently timed rationales**, not a rule to sell everything on every macro scare. [A16; A23; H125]

## 19. Say NO to an economically plausible but structurally unattractive short

- **Trigger/evidence:** borrowing/free float/buybacks, unlimited liability, squeezes and public conflict can overwhelm fundamental analysis; a regulatory thesis lacks an executable timetable.
- **Valuation:** cost-to-carry and price-path risk as well as eventual business worth; **sizing:** after 2018, no intended individual-stock short sleeve; historical puts explicitly cap loss but still consume premium; **catalyst:** historically regulatory/business deterioration; **exit:** capital/attention or structure no longer justify it.
- **Examples:** **Herbalife cash short → puts → close** (2017/2018 letters); **MBIA/bond-insurer protection** is the earlier different structure that worked; **2020 CDS indices** illustrate a retained alternative rather than reversal of the stock-short prohibition. **Boundary:** these are historical contrasts, not three examples of post-2018 stock shorting. [A14; A17; A18; A19]

## 20. Say NO until valuation and predictability align—even if the business is admired

- **Trigger/evidence:** quality is established but the offered price requires exceptional outcomes, or strategy uncertainty cannot be narrowed; preparation permits patience.
- **Valuation:** target return cannot be supported by ordinary plausible cash outcomes; **sizing:** zero is a real decision; **catalyst:** lower price or new proof, neither promised; **exit:** if already owned, sell/redeploy; if unowned, keep watchlist rather than force a purchase.
- **Examples:** **Meta** (A25 says years of watchlist followed by attractive entry); **Netflix 2022 versus 2026** (proof plus price changed); **Hilton 2026 exit** (continued admiration but hurdle failure); **S&P Global 2017 small position exit** (rising price prevented meaningful size, distinct from business rejection). **Discipline warning:** do not manufacture named “passes” on biotech/startups without a dated public refusal. [A17; A22; A25; H126; KP26]

## Replay safeguard

Examples above summarize documented reasoning; they are **not point-in-time data packs**. For a 2011 CP replay, do not supply the 2014 success narrative, a 2017 exit, or a 2026 generalization. Select only source records dated on/before the decision cutoff. Similarity of business model does not license importing a later trade's facts, management team, valuation or outcome.
