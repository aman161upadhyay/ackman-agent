# Bill Ackman: biography and evolution of the investment process

**Archive cutoff: 2026-10-25.** This is a dated research archive, not an instruction to apply today's rules to yesterday's decisions. Numbers below distinguish manager-wide assets, fund equity, gross assets including debt, and operating-company assets. **A profitable year is not a trade hit rate.** No consistent, independently defined, position-level hit-rate series was located; unknown is intentional.

## Biographical anchors and competing period labels

William Albert Ackman was born May 11, 1966; he graduated from Harvard College in 1988 and Harvard Business School in 1992. He co-founded Gotham with David Berkowitz, subsequently formed Pershing Square Capital Management, and became the public face of its concentrated, engaged investment strategy. [Britannica biography, accessed 2026-10-25](https://www.britannica.com/money/Bill-Ackman); [official biography, accessed 2026-10-25](https://www.pershingsquareinc.com/team/bill-ackman/).

**Dates need reconciliation, not silent harmonization.** Ackman's own 2021 shareholder letter says Gotham launched in **1992**; some contemporary/secondary biographies describe a 1993 start. Pershing Square's official timeline separates **December 2003 formation of the adviser** from **January 2004 commencement of investing**. [A21; official timeline](https://pershingsquareinc.com/about-us/).

The requested “Pershing Square 1.0, 2004–2015” is a useful broad era, **not Ackman's own exact taxonomy**. In the 2014 annual letter he called the early structural-catalyst strategy 1.0 and the GGP/HHC-led transition to deeper board/operational engagement **2.0**. In the Winter 2015 Graham & Doddsville interview, he used a different four-step vocabulary: statistical cheapness → quality → activism → replacing mediocre managers. Do not treat these independently used version numbers as an official chronology. [A14; GD15]

## 1. Gotham: classical value, increasingly illiquid control (1992–2003)

**What changed:** statistical cheapness evolved toward quality and intervention, but the capital structure did not match the liquidity of some investments. The failed golf-course/control investment and redemption demands are a lesson in *funding*, not proof that valuation work alone was wrong. December 27, 2002 liquidation announcement and 2003 wind-down are described by Britannica; contemporary NYT documents the inability to dispose of assets without depressing prices. [Gotham sources above]

**His later account:** at Oxford on October 29, 2013, he emphasized doing diligence before wiring money and learning from failure. At Graham & Doddsville in Winter 2015 he described Gotham as a training ground for Pershing Square rather than claiming a flawless first record. These are **retrospective explanations**, not contemporaneous 1992 mandates. [Oxford report, 2013-11-05](https://www.businessinsider.com/ackman-at-oxfords-said-business-school-2013-11); GD15.

## 2. Activist expansion: structural catalysts become operating engagement (2004–2015)

**Initial method:** acquire a discounted minority stake and press for a discrete transaction—Wendy's/Tim Hortons separation or Ceridian sale. The later GGP restructuring led to board involvement, creation of HHC, recruitment of management and design of incentives. CP, Air Products and other campaigns extended this approach to operational performance. [A14]

**His words, 2014 annual letter, published 2015:** “Our holding periods were shorter.” The surrounding discussion says earlier campaigns required repeatedly recycling capital into new ideas; the later approach sought larger multiples of money over longer holdings. He did **not** say that every investment required a proxy contest, and cited 3G-controlled Restaurant Brands as a case where existing governance made a Pershing board seat unnecessary. [A14]

**Scale and permanent capital:** PSH's October 2014 listing reduced dependence on redeemable private funds. Yet by 2015 roughly 60% of equity was still in private funds, whose quarterly redemption structure imposed a roughly one-year capital “half-life.” The growing marketing burden competed with research time. This diagnosis was written in March 2022 and must be labeled hindsight. [A21]

**Fault line already visible:** JCP demonstrated that an impressive new CEO was insufficient without alignment and execution; the October 2013 letter explicitly called turnarounds inherently risky. Valeant's initially passive investment subsequently tested assumptions about platform/acquisition value, management and leverage. [Q313; A16]

## 3. Valeant/Herbalife crisis and organizational reset (2015–2018)

**What failed:** Valeant required too much confidence in prior management's acquisition and operating decisions; regulatory/reputational exposure and debt made a falling price more than harmless quotation volatility. Herbalife exposed the difference between an economic/regulatory thesis and an executable short with limited float, buybacks and powerful opposing holders. The 2017 letter discusses conversion to puts and the ultimate close. [A16; A17]

**Correction, not a claim that the stocks could never recover:** the 2016 annual letter explained Valeant was only about 3% of PSH at sale; even a large recovery could not justify the attention consumed. In March 2022 he explicitly acknowledged the subsequently profitable counterfactual of holding Valeant and Herbalife, while arguing redeployment performed better. [A16; A21]

**Four reset pillars in his retrospective:** exit troubled positions; shrink to an investment-centric organization; stabilize capital through greater insider ownership of PSH and stop fundraising for private funds; recommit to core business-quality principles. January 2018 presentation: “Growth will come from returns, not asset gathering”. Investment professionals stopped routine investor meetings; Ben Hakim assumed that responsibility. [A21; 2018 presentation above]

**Rule change:** 2018 annual letter: “we have no current intention of shorting stocks in the future.” This is a dated policy on company equity shorts, **not** a prohibition on CDS, rate swaptions or other asymmetric hedges. [A18]

## 4. Quieter engaged ownership and durable compounders (2018–2023)

**What persisted:** active ownership and willingness to improve operations. **What diminished:** public proxy warfare as the default route. Lowe's, Starbucks, Hilton, Agilent and UMG show quality, recurring cash generation, sensible governance and patient ownership; ADP and Chipotle bridge the earlier and later styles. In the 2023 annual letter: “We greatly prefer our quieter and more time-efficient approach to engaged long-term ownership.” [A18; A19; A21; A23]

**Capital permanence became part of the edge:** by the 2021 annual report nearly 90% of assets were in PSH and employees/affiliates owned more than 25% of PSH. Vivendi reportedly selected Pershing as UMG's anchor partly because it could remain a long-term owner without a compulsory exit. [A21]

**Macro is a separate, opportunistic sleeve, not constant market timing:** pandemic CDS monetized in March 2020; interest-rate options in 2021–22 protected against inflation; long-duration Treasury short exposure in 2023 expressed a different structural inflation view. A23 also records losses from 2023 hedges; do not train only on the spectacular winners. A dedicated subset of the team systematized the search for asymmetric opportunities after 2020. [A19; A21; A23; CNBC, 2023-09-28](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/09/28/cnbc-transcript-pershing-square-capital-management-ceo-bill-ackman-speaks-with-cnbcs-scott-wapner-live-during-the-cnbc-delivering-alpha-conference-today.html).

**Boundary test:** Netflix's April 2022 exit was not mere intolerance of volatility; the prospective range of business outcomes widened beyond the predictability required for a concentrated holding. Alphabet in 2023 was different: market fear of AI was considered disproportionate to a durable franchise. [A22; A23]

## 5. Platform ambitions, renewed capital raising and a louder public persona (2024–2026)

### Investment process

The quality/watchlist method continued, but the opportunity set expanded: Nike and Brookfield in 2024; Uber, Amazon, Hertz and Meta disclosures in 2025; Microsoft and new Visa, Mastercard, Netflix, S&P Global, ICE and Alcon investments in 2026. These are not all identical “buy the dip” cases: Nike/Hertz involve substantial operating repair; Alphabet/Uber require explicit AI/AV competitive analysis; payment networks involve network economics; Fannie/Freddie require legal/political scenario valuation. [A24; H125; A25; H126]

Portfolio rotation is real: CP exited April 2025 because its tariff/macroeconomic sensitivity was greater than the rest of the portfolio; Chipotle and Nike exited November 2025; Hilton February 2026 because valuation reduced prospective returns below the firm's hurdle despite continuing admiration for the company. In the 2025 annual letter, Hilton's earnings multiple rose from about 23x at entry to 32x at exit. These exits refute both “never sell quality” and “every sale means the business failed.” [H125; A25]

The February 2026 deck's HHH-inclusive AUM uses market capitalization **plus net debt** for HHH; it is not interchangeable with manager fee-paying AUM or investible PSH equity.

**A significant reversal in business strategy, not necessarily stock-selection philosophy:** 2018 deliberately stopped asset gathering; 2024–26 deliberately expanded the permanent-capital platform. In September 2026 Ackman described HHH as a “modern-day Berkshire Hathaway” and explained reallocating real-estate cash into insurance and other assets. Growth ambition should be modeled separately from the legacy fund's investability checklist. [A21; A24; PSI26; KP26]
