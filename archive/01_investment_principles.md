# Investment principles: dated statements, operational meaning, exceptions

**Cutoff: 2026-10-25.** Quotes are short source excerpts; prose outside quotation marks is research synthesis. “Usually” is not “always.” Later explanations are labeled retrospective. Source IDs resolve to URLs and dates below; companion letters archive provides fuller context.

## 1. The eight principles: preserve the actual checklist, not a synthetic quotation

There is no single universally numbered eight-item formulation. The **2026-02-11 official presentation** reproduces a **January 19, 2018** core-principles slide and shows the current eight headings below. Dominant position/high barriers are grouped together; minimal capital-markets dependency has its own heading. Catalysts are an additional implementation lever, not one of these eight headings. The website's current strategy uses the same broad framework. These are **institutional slide quotations**, not a transcript of eight sentences spoken by Ackman. [P26; Official]

| Principle: exact short heading | Operational question / evidence | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| “Simple, predictable, free-cash-flow-generative business” | Can an analyst explain recurring cash generation and its durable drivers, rather than merely forecast the stock? | A business need not be technologically primitive; Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft later qualify in his assessment. |
| “Formidable barriers to entry” | Brand, scale, network, installed base, distribution, intellectual property, unique assets; assess disruption as well as incumbency. | A large market share today is not proof the moat survives AI. |
| “Limited exposure to extrinsic factors that we cannot control” | Stress rates, commodities, regulation, currency, refinancing, politics and the cycle; do these dominate the thesis? | Fannie/Freddie and macro instruments are explicit exceptions, requiring distinct sizing/scenarios. |
| “Strong financial profile” | Sustainable margins, resilient capital structure and sufficient liquidity through stress. | Not a literal zero-debt rule; asset-light franchise systems and PSH itself can use considered debt. |
| “Minimal capital markets dependency” | Can the company survive without a favorable next equity/debt financing? | Avoid confusing a solvent underlying franchise with a solvent security at the wrong place in the capital structure. |
| “Large market capitalization” | Can a meaningful fund stake be accumulated and exited without excessive market impact? | A small percentage of a huge issuer can be a large percentage of fund NAV; issuer ownership and portfolio weight differ. |
| “Attractive valuation” | Compare price with current and optimized intrinsic value; require return potential and downside protection. | Quality does not make any price acceptable. |
| “Exceptional management & governance” | Integrity, relevant operating track record, incentives, board willingness to act; management can be in place or identified. | His activist toolkit can supply better leadership; do not require existing management already to be excellent in every activist entry. |

**All rows:** P26, “Commitment to Core Principles” and current eight-headings slide; Official. The P26 footnote says investments may have **some or all** of the characteristics. Do not turn a marketing checklist into a false claim that no exception has ever been made.

**Catalyst overlay:** A14 says: “We often seek investments where we can effectuate positive change to catalyze the realization of value.” That is “often,” not a requirement to replace management in every purchase. The same letter explicitly accepts Restaurant Brands' existing 3G governance. [A14]

## 2. What is a great business? What is intrinsic value?

**Great business, synthesis:** durable, understandable earning power; high returns on incremental capital; cash production beyond reinvestment needs; defensible competitive position; ability to maintain pricing and reinvest rationally. It is the underlying business, not just impressive historical stock appreciation or a celebrity manager. A14 emphasizes modest/negative leverage and avoiding dependence on external finance. GD15 distinguishes business quality from analyst cleverness. [A14; GD15]

**Intrinsic value, exact definition:** “The value of anything, I would say, other than love, let’s say, is the present value of the cash you can take out of it over its life.” — Ackman, Lex Fridman #413, February 20, 2024, investing-basics segment. This is a discounted distributable-cash definition, **not** simply book value, a comparable-company multiple, or next year's price target. [Lex24]

For **PSH itself**, Ackman calls NAV a conservative estimate of intrinsic value; that is an investment-holding-company application and should not be substituted for valuation of every operating company. [A18; A23]

## 3. Idea sourcing: watchlist plus dislocation plus a fixable gap

1. **Shareholder referrals and reputational equity:** GD15 attributes CP to an unhappy shareholder, Air Products to a happy CP shareholder with another underperformer, and Allergan to Valeant's approach. This is direct evidence, stronger than an unsupported claim that every idea begins with a quantitative screen. [GD15]
2. **Relative operating underperformance:** November 22, 2011 letter identifies CP's gap versus peers; the 2018 interim letter identifies Lowe's versus Home Depot. Look at margins, costs, sales productivity, returns, incentives, and *why the gap is fixable*. A cheap weak business is insufficient. [Q311; H118]
3. **Long-followed quality names repriced by temporary bad news:** January 26, 2022 Netflix entry letter cites a negative reaction to quarterly subscriber guidance; A23 discusses Alphabet's AI concerns; H125 discusses Amazon during tariff volatility. [NFLX-entry; A23; H125]
4. **Separations and neglected assets:** Wendy's/Tim Hortons; Fortune Brands/Beam; GGP/HHC. The idea may be to separate incompatible investor constituencies, not to forecast an industry boom. [A14; Q311]
5. **A prepared library, not impulsive dip-buying:** A25: “Our investment in Meta highlights our approach of closely following a watchlist of high-quality businesses and waiting patiently for an attractive entry price.” In the September 2026 Knowledge Project he describes two analysts doing a deep dive, filings/calls/expert networks, a model and deck, independent work by Ackman/Israel, and group discussion before commitment. [A25; KP26, 00:34:37–00:36:46]

**Unverified as a universal formulation:** “the stock is down a lot but the business is fine” is a useful paraphrase, not a verbatim quote established here. The question is whether intrinsic value survived, not whether price declined. A16's Valeant discussion demonstrates why that distinction matters. [A16]

## 4. Valuation: recurring economic earnings, discounted cash and prospective IRR

- **Normalize first:** GD15 describes converting GAAP into owner/economic earnings and then testing durability. Acquisition accounting, growth capex versus maintenance capex, temporary disruption, and economic versus reported EPS can materially change the answer. This normalization was part of his Valeant reasoning; its presence did not make that decision correct. [GD15; A16]
- **Project distributable cash:** estimate normalized margins/volumes, reinvestment required, pricing and growth; assess the present value over a long horizon. Use scenarios rather than a point target with false certainty. [Lex24; A22]
- **IRR and entry multiple are linked:** in September 2026 he describes putting research aside if a price such as 35x earnings makes a **20%-plus return ambition** hard to attain. This is a dated ambition, **not a proven fixed hurdle for every prior trade**. [KP26, 00:34:37–00:35:36]
- **FCF yield plus growth, not yield alone:** A25 separates valuable investment capex from a rise in maintenance capital needs; H126 compares estimated prospective EPS growth with current earnings multiples. Its August 2026 table estimates roughly 20% weighted growth at 19x earnings versus S&P 500 12% at 20x; these are **Pershing estimates**, and its growth column includes dividend yield. They are not subsequently realized returns or an objective industry consensus. [A25; H126]
- **Optimized value must not become unbounded platform value:** P26 states “Base case valuation not reliant on future M&A or platform value”. A16's Valeant postmortem rejects assigning substantial durable value merely to a manager's acquisition record. [P26; A16]
- **Strategic-buyer value is a transaction/scenario cross-check:** Allergan/Actavis, Beam/Suntory, and Landry's negotiated take-private show value realized through buyers. Do not replace DCF with “a buyer might pay more” absent identified buyer economics and probability. A universal verbatim definition of value as “what the business is worth to a strategic buyer” was **not verified** in the fetched sources. [A14; Q210]

## 5. Position sizing: loss budget, conviction, liquidity and opportunity cost

Formal fund language: typically **8–12 core holdings**. GD15: “We size things based on how much we think we can make versus how much we think we can lose.” In that interview he said the fund would probably tolerate 5–6% capital at risk in a single investment and illustrated roughly 20% downside on a 27%-of-capital Allergan investment. Fannie/Freddie was described as a roughly 2–2.5% position because complete loss was possible. **These are Winter 2015 examples, not permanent risk limits.** [GD15; A21]

**10–20% is a description of many core positions, not a universal rule.** GD15 says the firm wanted 10% or more in an investment; Allergan was larger; probabilistic positions smaller. A19 records trimming Chipotle by one-third after it exceeded 20% of the portfolio. A22 says size changes generally manage concentration rather than represent trading around a position. [GD15; A19; A22]

Liquidity is central: A14 discusses building a full position after research rather than a token research stake, while often preserving capacity to add; A17 explains that rising prices can prevent sufficient scale. “Size reflects conviction and liquidity” is **synthesis**, not a verified standalone Ackman quotation. Explicitly stress permanent loss, correlation, downside scenario, issuer liquidity, fund funding terms and attention cost. [A14; A17; A16]

## 6. Activism toolkit and when not to use it

| Lever | Evidence / examples | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Board seats and governance | GGP/HHC, CP, Air Products; 2019 interim explains recruiting leadership and ongoing oversight. | Board membership constrains trading and consumes time. |
| CEO replacement / recruiting | Hunter Harrison at CP; Seifi Ghasemi at APD; Brian Niccol at Chipotle. | Relevant prior success matters; JCP warns against assuming skills transfer automatically. |
| Spin-offs / divestitures | Wendy's/Tim Hortons; GGP/HHC; Fortune Brands/Beam; APD/Versum. | Check stranded cost, debt allocation, taxes and each entity's financing needs. |
| Capital allocation | Buybacks, reducing excess cash, asset sales, avoiding destructive mergers. | Higher leverage or a financial transaction is not automatically value creation. |
| Proxy contest and public pressure | CP; Target; ADP. | Failed elections occur; public argument is an escalation tool, not the default investment thesis. |
| Constructive/private dialogue | ADP was first approached privately; A23 favors quiet engaged ownership. | Do not assume no influence merely because no public campaign exists. |
| No activism needed | 3G-governed Restaurant Brands; GD15/A14 permit excellent existing governance. | Buying a great business passively is consistent with the process. |

Sources: A14, Q313, H117, H119, A23. **Table is analytical synthesis**, not verbatim instructions.

## 7. Hedging and macro: mispriced insurance, not continuous beta suppression

**Exact policy:** “We do not use hedges to protect our mark-to-market performance from short-term downward volatility.” — A21, March 2022. A23 states the goal is to reduce permanent-loss risk and create liquidity when most valuable. A24 states the firm sat out meaningful hedges when prices were too high. [A21; A23; A24]

**Asymmetric means** limited premium/capital at risk, very large potential payoff if a mispriced event becomes probable, and an instrument whose contractual mechanics really express that view. “We only hedge when the cost is tiny relative to the payoff” is **paraphrase**; a verified A24 phrase is: “Without extraordinary opportunities, we sit tight.” [A21; A24]

| Decision, information date | Specific causal view and structure | Monetization / caution |
|---|---|---|
| Pandemic CDS, February–March 2020; described April 2020 | Shutdown could impair credit/equity; CDS indices were unusually cheap protection. Aggregate funds paid $27m premiums/commissions and received $2.6bn on exit; PSH proceeds $2.1bn. | Unwound March 12–23 as policy response/recovery outlook improved; reinvested largely in core equities. Do not call $27m an outright purchase of $2.6bn or attribute all proceeds to PSH. [A19] |
| Rate swaptions, initiated late 2020/2021; March 2022 account | Fiscal/monetary stimulus plus reopening → inflation and higher rates; option-like loss limit protected against discount-rate shock. | Chairman's A21 account gives $157m initial cost and $1.25bn proceeds across funds on sale of most exposure in January 2022; later strategies/realizations are separate. Re-established protection; no single blended trade P&L should be invented. [A21; 2022 presentation] |
| Long-duration Treasury short, public August–October 2023 | Structural inflation and inadequate yield compensation; CNBC interview discusses 30-year fixed-price contract risk. | Covered October 23 citing geopolitical/economic risk. A23 reports **overall 2023 hedging losses of 187bp**, including energy and Japanese-rate losses partly offset by USD-rate gains. A profitable high-profile bond bet is not proof every hedge won. [CNBC23; cover23; A23] |

**Macro tension:** November 2008 letter calls short-term economic prognostication largely futile and seeks businesses less exposed to extrinsic variables. Later macro trades are **opportunistic exceptions where view plus instrument plus price align**, not evidence that valuation always begins with predicting GDP. Tariff sensitivity contributed to CP's 2025 exit even while the franchise remained admired. [Q308; H125]

## 8. Sell discipline: five distinct reasons

1. **Price closes the value gap / hurdle falls:** YUM (Q2 2010); Nike's earlier 2017 investment, up 34% over four months; Hilton February 2026. A17: “we are always willing to redeploy capital if an investment appreciates to a level that no longer offers sufficient returns relative to other potential opportunities.” [Q210; A17; A25]
2. **Better risk-adjusted use of capital:** 2010 letter explicitly allows selling before fair value; Berkshire 2020 exit reallocates to opportunities judged more compelling. Its August 2020 letter discusses the sale; do not infer that Ackman repudiated Buffett's entire philosophy. [Q110; H120]
3. **Loss of predictability / thesis broken:** Netflix April 2022, despite continued admiration and acknowledgment management might succeed. Re-entry in 2026 followed actual evidence on ads/cash generation and a better price, not a denial of the original uncertainty. [A22; NFLX-exit; H126; KP26]
4. **Poor return on attention/capital:** Valeant March 2017; small remaining portfolio weight and continuing demands on human resources. The sell decision need not imply expected stock return is negative. [A16]
5. **Changed risk environment or excess concentration:** CP April 2025 tariffs; Chipotle 2020/2025 trims; UMG 2025 trim. Distinguish a partial risk-control sale from full thesis abandonment. [A19; H125]

No price-only mechanical stop loss was verified. A loss of confidence in forecastability can compel sale at a large loss; a large price decline with intact economics can instead justify additions. [A14; A22]

## 9. What he avoids—and the exceptions a faithful agent must retain

| Avoid / decline trigger | Evidence and qualification |
|---|---|
| **Public individual-stock shorts after Herbalife** | A18 policy; CNBC23 confirms no stock shorting. Do not prohibit asymmetric macro derivatives. |
| **Financing dependency / margin-driven forced sales** | Core checklist and Gotham lesson. PSH's fixed-term investment-grade debt is not a margin loan; its later leverage is openly disclosed. |
| **Unforecastable business outcomes** | Netflix 2022; AI disruption analysis. A familiar brand does not make future earnings predictable. |
| **Turnaround without governance alignment or relevant CEO experience** | Q313 says turnarounds require an aligned board, experienced CEO and stakeholder confidence. **Not an absolute “no turnarounds without legal control” rule**: CP, Chipotle, Lowe's, Nike and Hertz disprove that literalization. |
| **Uncompensated binary political/regulatory risk** | Fannie/Freddie are deliberate exceptions, sized as possible total-loss investments in GD15. Not a blanket prohibition. |
| **Speculative technology lacking durable cash economics** | Business-quality filter, not a sector ban. Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Uber and 2026 Netflix are direct counterexamples to “never tech.” |
| **Biotech / unpredictable product outcomes** | A broad core-filter implication; **no verified universal verbatim sector ban located**. Do not fabricate an explicit permanent biotech exclusion. |
| **Acquisition-platform value without defensible existing earnings** | Valeant postmortem; P26 explicitly excludes reliance on future M&A in base case. HHH's later ambitions must be evaluated on separately dated evidence. |
| **Good business at an inadequate prospective return** | Watchlist/Meta; 2026 Hilton sale; 2026 Knowledge Project library example. |

Sources: A14, Q313, A16, A18, GD15, A25, H125, H126, P26, KP26, CNBC23.

## 10. Concentration and permanent capital

Concentration allocates capital only to best-understood, high-conviction businesses and permits deep monitoring; it does not eliminate mistakes or eccentric annual returns. Closed-end capital removes investor redemption-driven forced sales, allowing falling prices to be useful rather than existential. It also permits long-term company relationships. **It does not eliminate fund-share discount risk, leverage risk, business impairment or governance conflicts.** [A14; A18; A21; A23]

PSH differs from holding the underlying stocks directly: fees, debt, cash, hedges and NAV discount matter. By 2026, manager economics, PSUS, PSH and HHH introduced additional layers; one must identify the actual issuer/vehicle before applying an ownership thesis. [A25; H126; PSI26]

## 11. Buffett/Munger influence without ventriloquism

Lex24 attributes price/value, margin of safety and the weighing-machine framing to Graham/Buffett; Ackman identifies Buffett's letters as formative. GD15 uses owner earnings and quality/durability. Permanent capital and HHH's insurance/equity-investment model borrow explicitly from Berkshire. **Do not attribute every famous Buffett or Munger saying to Ackman, or imply Buffett endorsed his individual investments.** [Lex24; GD15; A21; KP26]

A specific independently verified Ackman quotation about **Munger's** influence was not located in this pass. His influence should not be expanded into a fabricated bibliography or claimed meeting. [Research limitation, 2026-10-25]

## 12. Mistakes and actual rule changes

| Case | What failed in Ackman's account | Discipline learned / limits of inference |
|---|---|---|
| **Target / Target IV** | Special-purpose concentrated exposure and losses are part of the record; early shareholder letters and contemporary Target proxy materials require separate vehicle analysis. | Exact permanent “never options again” rule **unverified**, and later options use would contradict it. Do not invent a post-Target prohibition. |
| **JCP (2013 exit)** | Governance/CEO misalignment and turnaround execution. | Q313: “Turnarounds are inherently risky and require a totally aligned board of directors, a CEO with substantial turnaround experience, and the support and confidence of all stakeholders.” This is not a ban on every retailer. |
| **Valeant (2017 exit)** | Too much dependence on acquisition execution/management; underestimated political/regulatory and reputational risk; position too large for those risks. | Do not capitalize acquisition magic; stress second-order intrinsic-value damage and attention costs. A16 contains damaged OCR in several bullet passages, so clean paraphrases are not presented as verbatim text. |
| **Herbalife (2018 close)** | Thesis persistence did not overcome adverse float/technical dynamics and the costs of a public short battle. | A17 emphasizes technical factors; A18 abandons future company stock shorting. A correct critique of a business is not necessarily an attractive executable short. |
| **Netflix (2022)** | New information widened prospective outcomes beyond the concentrated strategy's certainty requirement. | Exit promptly; do not wait to break even. 2026 re-entry shows a failed earlier trade does not permanently blacklist the issuer. |

Sources: [Target campaign letter filed at SEC, April 24, 2009](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1275323/000095012309007175/y01431d4dfan14a.htm); Q313, A16, A17, A18, A22, H126, KP26. The Target campaign exhibit is not a Target IV investor letter: exact Target IV loss percentages and postmortem rules remain unverified.
